Working Papers

  • Do Jobs Created by Foreign Firms Decrease Support for an Anti-Globalization Politician?

    ByungKoo Kim, Seungjun Kim, and Jong Hee Park

    Studies have shown that job losses from economic globalization in the US have fueled anti-globalization sentiment and support for anti-establishment candidates. However, these studies often overlook that the US has been the largest recipient of foreign direct investment (FDI). We argue that residents of FDI-rich counties experiencing local economic booms and enhanced economic well-being are less likely to support anti-globalization political candidates. Analyzing county-level data from 2008 to 2020 in the US, we show that counties with significant FDI job growth exhibited lower vote swing for Donald Trump in the 2016 presidential election compared to those receiving less significant FDI-led job growth. This mitigating effect was more pronounced in counties with higher job losses, particularly among white and male populations, and in rural areas. Placebo tests using non-FDI job growth further demonstrate that it is specifically FDI-led employment growth, rather than general employment growth, that uniquely reduces support for anti-globalization platforms.

  • Delivering Against Populism: How Infrastructure Investment Undermines Extremist Politics in High-Need Districts

    Stefano Jud, ByungKoo Kim, Seungjun Kim, and Jong Hee Park

    Can governments counter populist movements through public goods provision? We examine this question using Biden's Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act and the 2022 US congressional elections. Combining district-level infrastructure spending data with election results and individual-level survey data from the 2022 Cooperative Election Study, we test whether targeted public investment can reduce support for populist candidates. We find that infrastructure spending reduced Republican vote share, but this effect was dramatically amplified in districts where (1) infrastructure needs were highly visible (poor bridges) and (2) Trump endorsed the Republican candidate. In high-need districts, infrastructure spending reduced vote share for Trump-endorsed candidates by approximately 5 percentage points more than non-endorsed candidates. This pattern reveals a fundamental tension in populist politics: populist leaders oppose government spending ideologically, even when their constituents desperately need public goods. Our findings suggest that well-targeted public goods provision can effectively counter populist movements by exposing the gap between populist rhetoric and material delivery, contributing to debates about how democracies can respond to the populist challenge.
